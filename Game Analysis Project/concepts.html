Concepts & Theory

Applying FMS 365 course concepts to Life Is Strange: True Colors.

Main Argument

Life Is Strange: True Colors is not just a story about empathy. It turns empathy into an interactive system. The player practices emotional interpretation through mechanics, dialogue, exploration, and choice.

Applying Course Concepts

Rogers: Mechanics, Goals, and Systems

Rogers explains games through basic design elements such as mechanics, rules, goals, and player actions. In True Colors, the most important mechanic is Alex’s empathy power. This mechanic organizes how players explore, gather information, make decisions, and progress through the story.

Koster: Games Teach Patterns

Koster argues that games are engaging because they teach players patterns. True Colors teaches emotional and social patterns. Players learn how characters respond, what emotions reveal, and how choices may affect relationships.

Steinkuehler: Games as Cultural Texts

Steinkuehler argues that games are worth studying because they are cultural spaces where players engage with identity, systems, and meaning. True Colors fits this approach because it explores grief, community, trauma, and belonging through interactive play.

Crawford: Interactivity as Exchange

Crawford argues that interactivity requires more than clicking buttons. It involves a meaningful exchange between the player and the system. Alex’s empathy power creates this exchange because players observe emotions, interpret them, choose responses, and receive feedback.

Salen & Zimmerman: Games as Spaces of Possibility

Salen and Zimmerman describe games as spaces of possibility. True Colors creates possibility through dialogue choices, optional interactions, and relationship outcomes. The player cannot change everything, but each decision changes how the story is experienced.

Bycer: Agency and Emergent Gameplay

Bycer’s discussion of agency helps explain the game’s choices. The player’s agency is not unlimited, but it is meaningful. Players shape relationships, tone, trust, and emotional consequences, even when the larger story remains structured.

Picucci: Narrative Architecture

Picucci’s model of video game narrative architecture explains how stories are built across different parts of a game. In True Colors, story appears through cutscenes, conversations, environmental details, memories, and optional exploration.

Domsch: Video Game Narrative Forms

Domsch explains that video game stories are told through multiple forms, including cutscenes, conversation sections, and interactive spaces. True Colors uses all of these methods to make the player an active participant in the story.

Miller: Characters, Dialogue, and Emotion

Miller’s work helps explain Alex as a player character. Alex is not a blank avatar; she has a specific history, personality, and emotional struggle. The player guides her choices, but Alex remains a defined character, creating tension between player agency and character identity.

Morrison and Narian: Meaningful Choice

Morrison argues that choices feel meaningful when players care about the outcomes. Narian’s categories of player choice help identify the types of decisions offered in the game, including moral, relationship, emotional, and narrative choices. True Colors uses these choices to make the player feel responsible for Alex’s relationships and emotional journey.

“The strongest part of True Colors is that empathy becomes playable: it is a mechanic, a narrative device, and a form of player agency.”
